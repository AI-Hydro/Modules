Choose a gauge below, then click Run ▶. The cell holds five water years of real USGS daily values (1 October 1995 to 30 September 2000) taken from the CAMELS-US v1.2 archive, so it runs offline and always gives the same answer. 4 · Reading the annual regime

  

    The lower panel of the plot shows the annual regime — mean discharge for each calendar month,
    averaged over the five water years. In the bundled record the four gauges look like this (values computed from the data):
  


  

    

      🌊 Fish River (ME) — mean flow peaks in May (144.8 m³/s) and April (129.2 m³/s) and is lowest in August (13.9 m³/s);
      all 92 days above P95 fall in April and May. A snowmelt-driven spring regime, consistent with CAMELS snow fraction 0.31.
    

    

      🌧️ Siletz River (OR) — mean flow peaks in December (136.3 m³/s) and is lowest in August (3.4 m³/s); 84 of the 92 days above P95
      fall in November to February. A winter-rain regime (snow fraction 0.03).
    

    

      🏜️ Sabino Creek (AZ) — zero flow on 704 of 1 827 days (38.5%) and a peak of 40.2 m³/s against a mean of 0.31 m³/s.
      Days above P95 occur in February–April (72 days) and again in July–September (19 days), so both winter storms and the summer monsoon matter.
    

    

      🌿 Leaf River (MS) — mean flow is highest from January to April (51–57 m³/s) and lowest in September (3.6 m³/s);
      90 of the 92 days above P95 fall in December to April. Strongly seasonal, not flat.
    

  


  

    What to look for
    After running the cell, compare the regime bar chart to these descriptions.
    Compare the intermittent, spiky Sabino Creek record with the smooth seasonal curve of the Fish River: the shape tells you how quickly the catchment responds and how much storage buffers rain.
  









  
5 · Spotting a flood event

  

    In the cell output, flood days are shaded amber and hatched (so they do not rely on colour) — days where discharge exceeds the
    95th percentile threshold of the whole record. This is a simple but robust
    peak-over-threshold (POT) approach used in operational flood studies.
  


  

    

      
Why P95?

      

        The 95th percentile means only 5% of all days exceed it. On this 1 827-day record that's
        about 91 days (92 once ties are counted) — spread across several distinct events. Lowering the threshold captures
        minor high-flow days; raising it isolates only extreme events.
      

    

    

      
Return period

      

        The cell ranks the five annual maxima (one per water year) and applies the Weibull plotting position:
        exceedance probability p = m/(n+1), return period T = (n+1)/m, with m the rank (Cunnane, 1978). With n = 5 the largest event gets
        T = 6 years and nothing beyond about 6 years can be supported — a short record cannot say how rare a flood is.
      

    

  


  

    Try this
    Switch the gauge to Sabino Creek (AZ) and re-run: days above P95 split into a winter–spring group and a July–September group.
    Compare that with Fish River (ME), where every one falls in April–May. This is regime diversity in action.
