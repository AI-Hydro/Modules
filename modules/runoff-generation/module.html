1 · Two ways rain becomes runoff

Two different conditions can make rain run over the ground instead of into it. Infiltration excess (Horton, 1933 [1]): rain arrives faster than the soil surface can take it, so the surplus ponds and runs off even though the soil below may be dry. Saturation excess (Dunne & Black, 1970 [2]): the soil store below is already full, so rain falling on that ground runs off however gently it falls. The two call for different explanations and different management, and a hydrograph on its own does not say which one made it.

SYNTHETIC Schematic only, not to scale. Left: the rate of rain i exceeds the infiltration capacity f of the surface. Right: the store (deficit D) is already filled, so F = D.

Why it matters Infiltration excess depends on the rate of the rain; saturation excess depends on the amount of water already stored. The rest of this lesson builds both as arithmetic you can move, then asks what real daily records can say about them.

2 · A soil column and its infiltration capacity SYNTHETIC

Green & Ampt (1911) [3] pictured a uniform soil with a sharp wetting front. With front suction ψ, an initial moisture deficit Δθ, and saturated conductivity Ks, the surface can take in water at a capacity that falls as the cumulative infiltration F grows:

capacity   f = Ks · (1 + ψΔθ / F)   →  Ks as F grows
constant rain i > Ks ponds when f falls to i:   Fp = KsψΔθ / (i − Ks),   tp = KsψΔθ / ( i (i − Ks) )   (Mein & Larson, 1973 [4])
after ponding:   G(F) = G(Fp) + Ks(t − tp),   G(F) = F − ψΔθ · ln(1 + F/ψΔθ)
if i ≤ Ks the surface never ponds, and runoff by this route is zero.

This SYNTHETIC column uses illustrative numbers chosen to make the effects visible, not a soil-texture table: Ks = 10 mm/h, ψ = 100 mm, Δθ = 0.30, so ψΔθ = 30 mm. Set a constant rain rate and a duration; the plot shows the rain, the falling capacity, and the water that cannot enter.

3 · A store that can fill SYNTHETIC

A real soil cannot take in water forever. Give the column a finite storage deficit D, the most water it can hold. While F < D, water that cannot enter is infiltration-excess runoff RIE; once F = D the store is full and all further rain is saturation-excess runoff RSE. Water is conserved:

P = i · T = F + RIE + RSE    (the column has no drainage from below in this SYNTHETIC simplification)

5 · The wet area grows SYNTHETIC

A hillslope is not one column. Along a hillslope, water collects downslope, so the storage deficit shrinks from the ridge toward the stream. This SYNTHETIC slope has 20 columns whose deficits fall from a large value at the ridge to a small one at the stream edge. A gentle rain (8 mm/h, below Ks) of the depth you choose falls on all of them. A column whose store fills becomes a saturation-excess source; the fraction of columns that fill is the saturated area. This is the idea behind variable-source-area models (Dunne & Black, 1970 [2]; Beven & Kirkby, 1979 [5]); the TWI lesson later builds the real map version.

6 · Real events, daily data REAL

  

    The first five sections were models. Here are observations: CAMELS-US v1.2 (Newman et al., 2015 [9]; Addor et al., 2017 [10]) daily USGS discharge and Daymet basin-mean precipitation
    (Thornton et al., 1997 [11]) for water years 1996–2000 (1827 days). For each rain event we ask what share of the rain left the basin as quickflow, the event runoff coefficient (RC;
    Merz et al., 2006 [6]; Blume et al., 2007 [7]).
  

  
event: a day with P ≥ 10 mm after 2 days with P < 1 mm (needs 7 earlier days of record)  ·  event rain = all rain from the start day to the window end

    window: from the start day for up to 10 days, or until the next event  ·  quickflow = Q − baseflow (Lyne–Hollick, α = 0.925, 3 passes) summed over the window

    RC = quickflow depth ÷ event rain depth  ·  antecedent = rain over the 7 days before the start

  
These thresholds are teaching choices, not standards; the table below shows how the median RC moves when the start threshold changes.
    The baseflow filter is the one used in the earlier hydrograph lessons (Nathan & McMahon, 1990 [12]; Ladson et al., 2013 [13]).
