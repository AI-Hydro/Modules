1 · A gauge is a point

A rain gauge is a funnel above a collecting tube. It reports the depth that fell on the funnel's opening, and nothing else. A hydrologist wants the depth over a whole basin. This lesson is about that jump from a point to an area, and then about what the area average tells you.

—

Why this matters Every basin-average precipitation is an inference from points (or from radar and satellite products, which have their own limits). How good it is depends on where the gauges sit relative to the rain; the next section tests that on a field you can inspect completely.

2 · From points to an area SYNTHETIC

Everything in this section is made up. A 60 × 60 grid of 1 km cells holds a storm total: 5 mm of background, an eastward orographic gradient of 0.30 mm per km, and one Gaussian storm cell (30 mm at its peak, σ = 4.5 km) over a made-up basin. Six gauges, G1 to G6, sit on fixed cells. Because we built the field, we know the true basin mean; a real basin never offers that answer key, which is why §3 to §6 use records.

Three ways to average the gauges

Arithmetic mean: add the readings and divide by the number of gauges, P̄ = (1/n) Σ Pᵢ. It ignores where the gauges are.
Thiessen polygons (Thiessen, 1911) [1]: every cell belongs to its nearest gauge, and each gauge's weight is the share of the basin area that is closest to it, P̄ = Σ wᵢ Pᵢ with wᵢ = Aᵢ / A.
Inverse-distance weighting (Shepard, 1968) [2]: the value in every cell is a weighted mean of the gauges, with weights proportional to 1 / dᵖ, and the basin mean is the average of all the cell values. In the limit of a very large power p the nearest gauge takes everything (the Thiessen map); as p shrinks towards 0 every gauge gets the same weight (the arithmetic mean).

3 · How hard, how often REAL

  

    Now real basin-mean daily precipitation: Daymet values averaged over each CAMELS-US basin [3,4,5], for water years 1996 to 2000
    (1 October 1995 to 30 September 2000, 1827 days). Pick a basin; §3 to §6 follow your choice. Three definitions are used throughout:
    a wet day has at least 1 mm; a high-precipitation day has at least five times the record's mean daily precipitation;
    a dry spell is a run of consecutive days each under 1 mm.

5 · Rain or snow? REAL

  

    A gauge total does not say whether the water arrived as rain or snow, but a thermometer helps. The simplest rule treats a day's precipitation as snow when the day's mean temperature,
    (Tmax + Tmin) / 2 from Daymet, is below a threshold. The threshold is a modelling choice, not a constant of nature: Jennings et al. (2018) [6] map how the rain–snow temperature threshold varies across the Northern Hemisphere.
    Move it for — and watch the snow fraction of the five-year precipitation depth respond.

Definitions
Wet day ≥ 1 mm; high-precipitation day ≥ 5 × the record's mean daily precipitation (exact integer test); dry spell = days under 1 mm in a row; snow day = daily mean temperature below the threshold. The lesson's own, for this record.
