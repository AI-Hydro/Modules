1 · What is a hydrograph?

A hydrograph is a time-series of discharge — usually m³/s on the y-axis and hours or days on the x-axis. Scroll the panel on the right to walk through the three named regions.

Step 1 · Rising limb

After rain falls, the channel doesn't respond instantly — water has to reach the stream. The discharge climbs steeply: this is the rising limb. Steeper limb → flashier catchment (small, urban, saturated).

Step 2 · Peak

The flow tops out at the peak discharge — the largest instantaneous flow during the event. Lag time is the delay between the rainfall centroid and this peak. For flood design, the peak is everything.

Step 3 · Falling limb (recession)

Once rain stops, the channel drains. The falling limb, or recession, often follows a near-exponential decay set by catchment storage. Slow recession → big groundwater reservoir; fast recession → little storage.

Why this matters The shape encodes catchment memory. Flashy = small / impervious / wet. Damped = large / forested / dry. It's the entry skill for flood forecasting, recession analysis, and model calibration.

3 · Baseflow separation — the Lyne–Hollick filter

Real baseflow isn't a straight line. The Lyne–Hollick filter [1,2] runs three passes (forward, backward, forward) of a one-parameter recursion, each pass filtering the baseflow left by the previous one (series reflected at both ends):

ft = α · ft−1 + (1+α)/2 · (Qt − Qt−1)
ft ≥ 0  ·  bt = max(Qt − ft, 0)

Drag α inline (or focus it and use the arrow keys): → a higher α lets quickflow persist longer, so more flow is classed as quickflow and the baseflow line is lower. BFI = baseflow volume ÷ total volume.

4 · Doubling rainfall ≠ doubling peak

Two storms on the same catchment. Drag the number (or focus it and use the arrow keys): drop as much rain on the right panel as the left, and the peak grows faster than linearly. In this model that comes from the curve-number loss: the first part of every storm (the initial abstraction) produces no runoff.

SYNTHETIC model: SCS curve number CN = 75 (USDA-SCS, 1972) gives S = 25400/CN − 254 = 84.7 mm and an initial abstraction Ia = 0.2·S = 16.9 mm. Runoff depth is Q = (P − Ia)² / (P − Ia + S) for P > Ia (equivalently (P − Ia)² / (P + 0.8·S)), else 0. That depth falls as an even 4-hour pulse on an assumed catchment area of 25 km², is routed with a gamma unit hydrograph (volume-conserving), and sits on a constant 2.2 m³/s baseflow. Real soils add moisture-dependent losses that a fixed CN does not capture.
